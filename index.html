Executive view

F.R.I.E.N.D. Proposal Overview

F.R.I.E.N.D. is designed as a division-level operating intelligence platform for King Soopers. Its purpose is to connect existing operational information, store routines, department execution, training, leadership coaching, and financial measurement into one structured decision-support model.

The proposal is intentionally staged. It does not ask leadership to immediately approve a full division or enterprise transformation. It asks for a professional pre-pilot, a controlled three-store pilot, a regional rollout, and then a division rollout only after measurable evidence supports expansion.

Executive thesis

The central operating claim is that King Soopers can reduce preventable loss, improve execution, strengthen leadership development, and increase associate clarity by converting fragmented retail information into guided daily action. A dashboard alone is not enough. The platform must include mission logic, accountability loops, training integration, and measurable behavior change.

How executives should evaluate this proposal
  • Does the pre-pilot create enough clarity to justify a controlled live pilot?
  • Does the pilot change leader and associate behavior?
  • Does the platform improve measurable operational outcomes?
  • Does the regional rollout prove the model can scale beyond three stores?
  • Does the full division rollout produce enough ROI to justify long-term support?
1. Pre-pilot

Pre-Pilot: Detailed Process Breakdown

The pre-pilot is the creation and validation phase before live store deployment. Its goal is to transform F.R.I.E.N.D. from a proposal into a disciplined, measurable, executive-ready operating model.

1. Executive alignment and sponsorship

The first requirement is to identify the executive sponsor, decision makers, pilot owners, and review cadence. This prevents the program from becoming an informal technology experiment.

  • Confirm King Soopers Division sponsorship.
  • Define who owns pilot approval, funding, operations, data, and measurement.
  • Define go/no-go decision gates before any live testing begins.
  • Determine whether the pre-pilot will be reviewed by store operations, finance, HR, IT, and legal.
2. Current-state workflow mapping

The pre-pilot should document how work currently flows through stores and departments. This includes markdowns, replenishment, labor movement, training, food safety checks, customer service, digital fulfillment, and leader routines.

  • Map department leader daily routines.
  • Identify which reports are used and when.
  • Identify repeated decision delays and duplicated work.
  • Document where training is disconnected from live execution.
3. Data-source inventory

F.R.I.E.N.D. depends on reliable operational signals. The pre-pilot must determine which data is available, how often it refreshes, who owns it, and whether it is reliable enough to influence daily decisions.

  • Sales and department performance data.
  • Shrink and markdown data.
  • Labor and schedule data.
  • Training completion and competency data.
  • Food safety and temperature logs.
  • Task completion and operational checklist data.
  • E-Commerce performance data, including pick accuracy and substitutions.
4. Dashboard and mission-engine prototype

The pre-pilot prototype should demonstrate how a store leader, district leader, department leader, and associate would interact with the platform.

  • Executive dashboard mockup.
  • Store composite score model.
  • Department intelligence pages.
  • Mission priority system: red, yellow, green.
  • Markdown and shrink opportunity logic.
  • Training prompts tied to live work.
5. Measurement design

The pre-pilot must define what success means before the pilot begins.

  • Baseline period: 8–12 weeks before pilot launch.
  • Pilot period: 3–6 months.
  • Primary metrics: shrink, markdown effectiveness, sales execution, labor efficiency, training completion, task completion, food safety, and adoption.
  • Secondary metrics: associate confidence, leader usefulness, speed of decision, and operating consistency.
6. Red team and risk review

Before the pilot begins, the proposal should be challenged by finance, operations, technology, HR, legal, and field leadership. The goal is not to weaken the proposal. The goal is to make it harder to dismiss.

3. Pilot program

3–6 Month Pilot Program Across Three Stores

The pilot should run for 3–6 months across three stores: one low-volume store, one medium-volume store, and one high-volume store. This structure tests whether F.R.I.E.N.D. can function in different operating environments before larger-scale investment.

Low-Volume Store

Tests simplicity, adoption burden, limited labor flexibility, and whether the platform reduces complexity for smaller store teams.

Medium-Volume Store

Tests the representative operating model most likely to reflect average division conditions.

High-Volume Store

Tests pressure, complexity, labor movement, department intensity, leader bandwidth, and scalability under demanding conditions.

Pilot phase 1: Baseline collection
  • Collect 8–12 weeks of pre-pilot baseline data.
  • Measure shrink, markdown effectiveness, sales execution, labor, training, food safety, task completion, and adoption readiness.
  • Document department routines and leader decision points.
  • Identify which performance gaps are recurring versus isolated.
Pilot phase 2: Program integration
  • Connect approved operational data feeds.
  • Create store leader, department leader, district leader, and division review views.
  • Configure department-specific mission logic.
  • Train pilot users on how to interpret and act on recommendations.
  • Confirm privacy, access, and data governance requirements.
Pilot phase 3: Live operating use
  • Daily mission review by store and department leaders.
  • Weekly pilot review with leadership.
  • Track alert-to-action time and mission completion.
  • Compare markdown completion and shrink indicators against baseline.
  • Collect associate and leader feedback weekly.
Pilot phase 4: Data collection and analysis
  • Measure financial indicators and operating behavior changes.
  • Compare pilot stores to baseline and comparable non-pilot stores.
  • Separate eligible markdown categories from non-eligible categories.
  • Identify data gaps, false alerts, adoption barriers, and training needs.
Pilot phase 5: Executive review
  • Present pilot adoption results.
  • Present operating metric changes.
  • Present financial opportunity range.
  • Present red team findings.
  • Recommend stop, revise, extend, regional rollout, or division rollout.
